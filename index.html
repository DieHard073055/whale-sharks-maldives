01 · Biology

A shark the size of a bus — and it eats plankton

The whale shark (Rhincodon typus) is the largest fish on Earth. Despite the name it is a shark — a slow, filter-feeding relative of rays and reef sharks — and it is completely harmless to people.

Whale sharks roam warm tropical seas, following plankton blooms and fish-spawning events. The Maldives is one of the very few places on the planet where they live year-round.

02 · Anatomy

Built to strain the sea

  • A mouth up to 1.5 m wide — set at the very front of the head, unlike most sharks, so it can scoop water as it swims.

  • Tiny teeth, working gills — hundreds of rows of small teeth go almost unused; gill rakers sieve plankton from thousands of litres of water an hour.

  • Armoured skin — among the thickest of any animal (up to ~10 cm), studded with tooth-like denticles, even over the eyeballs.

  • It can't blink — no eyelids; a whale shark may roll its eyes away when something gets too close. One more reason to keep your distance.

03 · Behavior

Slow cruiser at the surface, deep diver below

Cruising

Steady swimming at roughly 3–5 km/h, often just under the surface — slow enough for a snorkeler to fin alongside without chasing.

Feeding

Mouth-open ram feeding through plankton-rich water; where prey is dense the shark may stop and hang vertically, pumping water through its mouth. In South Ari, surface feeding appears in only about 16% of encounters — most sharks you meet are simply travelling.

Diving

Whale sharks regularly dive hundreds of metres — recorded to nearly 1,900 m — probably to feed at depth, then return to the warm surface layer to reheat. What you see from a snorkel is only the top of their day.

Sources: MWSRP research overview; depth record reported in satellite-tagging studies.

04 · The Maldives

South Ari Atoll: a year-round home for young giants

One of a kind

The South Ari Marine Protected Area (SAMPA, protected since 2009) is the only year-round whale shark aggregation of its kind — sightings are possible in every month. Nutrient-rich upwellings along the reef edge concentrate the plankton the sharks follow.

A nursery for young males

Researchers believe SAMPA is a "secondary nursery": the sharks you'll meet are mostly juveniles that move on as they approach maturity.

Locals with names

The whale shark is fehurihi in Dhivehi. One SAMPA regular, "WS071", has been re-sighted more than 380 times over 14 years.

When to go

Year-round sightings; the calmest seas and clearest water come with the dry northeast monsoon, December to April.

05 · In the water

What an encounter actually looks like

1The briefing.

On the boat: the rules, your entry position, where to look. Listening well makes the encounter better for you and the shark.

2The search.

The crew cruises the reef edge, scanning for a dark shadow or a fin and tail breaking the surface.

3The drop.

The boat moves ahead of the shark's path. Slide in quietly — no jumping, no splashing.

4The swim.

Fin alongside at mid-body and match its pace. Encounters last minutes; the boat may circle back for another drop.

5The moment.

The sheer scale, the remoras riding along, the spotted skin drifting past. Look first — photograph second.

05 · In the water — the rules

3 m from the body, 4 m from the tail

Do

  • Approach from the side, level with the mid-body
  • Slide into the water calmly — no jumping or splashing
  • Follow your guide's positioning and signals
  • Keep fins below the surface and movements smooth

Don't

  • Never touch or ride — it strips the shark's protective mucus and always ends the encounter
  • Don't block its path or cross in front of it
  • No flash photography
  • No scooters or motorized swimming aids

Boats keep at least 10 m away; maximum 12 swimmers per shark. Source: Maldivian Whale Shark Tourist Encounter Guidelines, EPA Maldives.

05 · In the water — practical notes

Before you jump in

On the day

  • It's a snorkel, not a dive. Encounters happen at the surface; scuba is not used around the sharks — bubbles disturb them.
  • Dress for the equator. Rash guard or leggings plus reef-safe sunscreen; the sun reflects straight off the water.
  • Seasickness? Take tablets before departure — trips head out onto open water along the reef edge.
  • Swimming fitness. Expect some current and surface chop. Ask for a life vest or float without hesitation — guides are used to it.

Worth knowing

  • Wild animals, no guarantees. South Ari offers year-round chances, but no honest operator promises a sighting.
  • Your quality check: a good operator briefs the rules first and never chases a shark that turns away.
  • The best photo is the left-side spot pattern behind the gills. Submit it to MWSRP's Big Fish Network — your shot may identify a new shark.
  • Pace yourself. Multiple drops are normal; hydrate and re-apply sunscreen between swims.

06 · Conservation

Endangered — and your visit can help

Status

Endangered on the IUCN Red List since 2016 — the global population is estimated to have fallen by more than half over 75 years.

Threats

Vessel strikes, entanglement and bycatch, plastic pollution — filter feeders swallow microplastics along with their plankton — and pressure from unregulated tourism.

Your part

Choose operators who brief and enforce the code of conduct. Keep your distance. Send your spot-pattern photos to MWSRP. Skip single-use plastic on local islands.

Sources: IUCN Red List; Cagua et al. 2014, whale shark tourism economics.
